How the Solver Thinks

A plain-language tour of the mathematics behind the capacity-constrained tessellation.

Written for someone who follows the ideas but does not want to push symbols around. Each section introduces one idea and says what it buys us. Every formula is restated in words directly beneath it. Read it top to bottom once; afterwards it works as a reference, and the glossary at the end is the lookup table.

1The cast

Sites are the points we move. Cells are their Voronoi regions: every point of the sphere belongs to the nearest site. Density ρ is a function on the sphere that says how “heavy” each patch of surface is. A cell’s capacity (or mass) is the integral of the density over it — how much weight it holds.

We want two things at once:

  • Centroidal: each site sits at the weighted centre of its own cell. This is what makes cells look compact and honest rather than skewed.
  • Equal capacity: every cell holds the same mass. With a density that varies, that means small cells where it is heavy and large cells where it is light. That is where the pattern comes from.

Everything below is about getting both, fast, for any density you care to hand us.

3Slope versus curvature

The gradient is the slope: which way is downhill, and how steeply. A method that uses only the gradient (gradient descent, and to a large extent L-BFGS, which builds a rough memory of curvature from past slopes) has to guess how far to step. In a long narrow valley it zig-zags.

The Hessian is the curvature: how the slope changes as you move. Knowing the curvature lets you do what Newton’s method does in one dimension — fit a parabola to where you stand and jump to its bottom:

Two practical notes about how we use it:

  • The Hessian for N sites is a 3N×3N table, but we never build it. We only ever need to answer “what is the curvature along this particular direction?”, which is a Hessian-times-vector product, and the conjugate-gradient solver inside each Newton step only ever asks that question. That is what lets it scale.
  • We do not trust the parabola blindly. A trust region is a radius within which we believe the quadratic sketch. Take the best step inside it; if the landscape dropped about as much as the sketch predicted, widen the radius; if it did not, shrink it and try again. A radius shrinking to nothing is the “stalled” outcome in the logs: the sketch keeps disagreeing with reality even for tiny steps.

The curvature of E turns out to have a clean form: it is built entirely from line integrals of the density along cell boundaries (the bisectors between neighbouring sites). File that fact away; it matters below.

The standard tool is the augmented Lagrangian, which turns the constrained problem into a sequence of unconstrained ones by modifying the landscape in two ways:

  • The penalty μ2∑ck2 is a spring: violate the constraint and the landscape rises around you. μ is the spring stiffness. A spring alone never quite reaches the road, and a very stiff spring makes the valley so narrow that slope-only methods crawl. That narrowing is the ill-conditioning that made L-BFGS need thousands of inner iterations; Newton sees the narrowness as curvature and is unbothered by it.
  • The multipliers λk are prices. λk is the exchange rate between energy and mass for cell k: how much E would improve if that cell were allowed slightly more mass than its share. With the right prices in place, the unconstrained minimum of L lands exactly on the road, no infinite stiffness required.

5Precision is the first enemy

Before smoothness or curvature can matter, the landscape has to be evaluated consistently. Newton compares “how much did L actually drop” against “how much did the parabola predict”, and near a solution those drops are tiny — around 10−10 on a landscape whose height is of order one. Any inconsistency in how L is computed that is larger than that reads as the landscape lying, and the trust region collapses.

The dangerous kind of inconsistency is not random rounding, which averages out, but a geometric tolerance: a rule like “treat two points closer than ε as the same point”. Such a rule is a discontinuity. As sites move, a configuration crosses the threshold and a sliver of a cell silently appears or disappears, and the objective jumps by an amount proportional to ε. A jump of 10−6 is invisible to the eye and fatal to a solver aiming at 10−8.

The principle that keeps this out: never rederive geometry from rounded points when the exact geometry is already known. An arc that is part of a bisector lies on a great circle whose normal is known exactly from the two sites; a clipped piece of it lies on the same circle; the piece that closes a clipped polygon lies on the clipping circle. Carry those normals through instead of recomputing them from endpoints, and near-degenerate pieces become harmless — their contribution scales with their length, and the only tolerances left are at the level of machine rounding.

The working test for this is direct: represent a smooth density two ways (one global polynomial, the same function stored as tiles) and check that every cell and every edge integrates to the same answer to around 10−12. If that holds, the representation is out of the way and what remains is genuinely about the mathematics.

6Smoothness, and why it matters

A function is C⁰ if it has no gaps, C¹ if it also has no kinks (its slope is continuous), C² if its curvature is continuous as well. The absolute value |x| is C⁰ but not C¹: there is a corner at zero. The function x|x| is C¹ but not C²: smooth to look at, but its curvature jumps from negative to positive at zero.

Newton’s method steers by curvature. Where curvature jumps, the parabola fitted on one side of the jump is wrong about the other side, so the predicted drop disagrees with the real one, the trust region shrinks, and you stall. Newton’s textbook guarantee — fast convergence near the solution — assumes the curvature varies continuously, with no jumps. L-BFGS is more tolerant because it only measures slopes, but a kinked curvature still misleads its memory.

So the question is: how smooth is our landscape, and what decides that?

7How the density’s smoothness becomes the landscape’s

Moving a site moves its bisectors. The change in anything integrated over a cell is, to first order, an integral along the moving boundary. So each time we differentiate with respect to the sites, a cell integral becomes a boundary integral, and a boundary integral — because the boundary is now moving through the density — picks up the density’s own derivative. One level of differentiation costs one level of depth into ρ:

QuantityDepends on
E, the mass errors ckρ integrated over cells
slope of E, slope of ckρ integrated along bisectors

curvature of ckthe gradient of ρ along bisectors, plus how the cell corners move

The curvature of E gets a free pass — it stays at “ρ on bisectors” because of the same cancellation that made its slope so simple.

Read the last row again. The curvature of the constraints involves the gradient of the density on the bisectors. If the density has kinks — continuous values but jumping slopes — then the constraint curvature jumps wherever a bisector crosses a kink, and the landscape L is C¹ but not C². That is exactly the situation with a density stored as polynomial tiles on a triangle mesh that match values at tile edges but not slopes.

And now the fix reads off the table: make the density C¹ — tiles that match slopes as well as values — and the constraint curvature is continuous, so L is C². Better still, a piecewise polynomial with a continuous gradient has a gradient that cannot change faster than some fixed rate (it is Lipschitz, in the jargon), which is precisely the condition Newton’s fast-convergence theorem asks for. We do not need C² tiles. C¹ is the whole ask.

The Gauss–Newton model keeps the first and third. The third is cheap, built from slopes we already have, and it is the term that grows with μ and governs conditioning. The two terms containing ∇2ck are the expensive ones: per the table, they need the gradient of ρ on bisectors and the velocities of the Voronoi corners as sites move.

Near a solution the fourth term dies on its own: the mass errors ck go to zero. The second does not. The prices λk settle to nonzero values — mass genuinely has a price when the density varies — so the Gauss–Newton model is permanently off by ∑λk∇2ck. That alone makes it a good quasi-Newton method rather than a true one: it still converges, but linearly rather than quadratically, and on a kinked density the missing term is exactly the one that jumps. The test for how much this costs is to feed the trust-region step the true curvature measured by finite differences — nudge the sites along a direction, re-evaluate the slope, divide by the nudge — and count iterations. When that halves the count, the term is worth deriving.

9How a noise field becomes tiles

This is the whole “discretise the function, not the space” idea made concrete. Passing -f noise does four things:

  1. Start with something we cannot integrate. The noise is a Perlin-style function evaluated anywhere on the sphere, floored into a range like 0.2–1.0 so density is never zero. It is smooth, but it has no formula that integrates in closed form.
  2. Lay a triangle mesh over the sphere. An icosahedron, each triangle split into four, four times over: 5 120 spherical triangles about 6° across. Every edge is a great-circle arc — the same kind of curve the cells are made of.
  3. On each triangle, replace the noise by the quadratic that agrees with it at six points. A quadratic on the sphere has six degrees of freedom, x2,y2,z2,xy,yz,zx, and because x2+y2+z2=1 on the sphere this family already contains the constants. Sample the noise at the three corners and three edge midpoints, solve a six-by-six system, and you have the one quadratic through those values. Neighbouring triangles share their samples, so tiles agree in value along shared edges — no gaps — but not in slope. Kinks.
  4. Now every integral is exact. A cell is bounded by great-circle arcs; a tile is bounded by great-circle arcs; their overlap is again such a polygon, and the tile’s quadratic integrates over it in closed form. The solver sums over the five to ten tiles a cell touches. The only approximation in the pipeline is step 3, made once, up front.

Why this is the right trade: the error in step 3 is a quadratic fitting a smooth function over a 6° patch — small — and, by the bandwidth rule, detail finer than a cell cannot move the cells anyway. At 200 sites a cell is about 8° across, so 6° tiles already resolve everything the tessellation can see; at 2 000 sites you would subdivide once or twice more. What you never do is make the mesh as fine as the noise.

The alternative, -f noise-fit, fits one global degree-8 polynomial to twenty thousand samples. It is perfectly smooth, and it is not a density: least squares constrains no value, so fitting a function with a floor overshoots below it. That fit reaches −0.047 where the noise floor is 0.2. Cells in a region of negative density have no mass to balance against the others, and the tessellation visibly distorts around them. Local tiles of low degree do not have this problem — each piece is built from samples of the function itself — and their one weakness, the kinks, is fixable.

10Which tiles, and why

A reminder of the contract that makes everything exact. A polynomial density integrated over a region bounded by great-circle arcs has a closed-form answer (Stokes’ theorem, applied recursively). Voronoi cells on the sphere are bounded by great-circle arcs. So as long as the density is polynomial — or polynomial on tiles whose own edges are great-circle arcs, so that a cell clipped to a tile is again such a region — every integral the solver needs is exact. No quadrature, no sampling error, no mesh of the space. What we discretise instead is the function: we replace the density you hand us with the nearest member of a family we can integrate exactly.

The simplest family is “polynomial per triangle, values agree at edges” — what you get by sampling the function at six points per triangle and fitting. Neighbouring pieces then share those samples, so the values match along the seams but the slopes do not: the field is continuous with kinks in it, and by the table in section 7 those kinks are what make the constraint curvature jump.

The family that removes them is “polynomial per triangle, values and slopes agree at edges” — a C¹ spline. The standard construction from quadratic pieces is the Powell–Sabin split: cut each triangle into six, and the nine numbers “value and gradient at each corner” then determine a quadratic on every sixth with matching slopes across every seam. The one subtle part is where to cut: the split point on a shared edge has to be where the great circle joining the two neighbouring triangles’ interior points crosses it. That placement is what makes the two sides agree on the direction the surface leaves the edge in; any other split point and no such spline exists.

Two things come free with it. First, the Bézier form writes each piece in terms of a few control values, and the piece is bounded below by the least of them — so “this density is positive everywhere” becomes a check on a list of numbers rather than a search over the sphere that might miss a dip. Second, when the mesh is too coarse to carry the gradients you sampled, the interpolant overshoots and can dip below zero; damping the gradients at the vertices pulls it back, and because a vertex is shared by every piece that touches it, the field stays exactly C¹ while it is brought back into range. The report says how much damping was needed, which is a direct measurement of whether the mesh resolves the field.

Because each sixth is still a polynomial on a great-circle triangle, none of this touches the integration path: a C¹ field is just a piecewise field built more carefully.

Measuring it was more interesting than building it. On a target that is itself smooth the C¹ field is more accurate than the simpler one at the same mesh, and at the same order. On the noise field it is less accurate — not because of the floor, where the target is constant and the spline is exact, but because the noise is rough at the mesh scale, and this construction reads one gradient per vertex where the simpler one also samples the edge midpoints. Once a target stops being smooth, sampling more places beats sampling derivatives once. And it did not make the solver faster: the clipping fault fixed earlier had already removed the stalls that motivated it, so there was less for smoothness to recover than expected.

11Glossary

Gradient / slope
The direction and rate of steepest increase. We step against it.
Hessian / curvature
How the slope changes as you move; a matrix, but we only ever use it as “curvature along a direction”.
Newton’s method
Fit a parabola using slope and curvature, jump to its bottom. Few steps, each more expensive.
L-BFGS
Walk downhill using slopes and a short memory of past slopes to approximate curvature. Cheap steps, many of them.
Trust region
The radius within which we believe the parabola; widened or shrunk by comparing predicted and actual drop.
Conjugate gradient
The inner linear solver that finds the Newton step using only curvature-along-direction questions.
Constraint
An equation the answer must satisfy — here, each cell’s mass equals its share.
Penalty, μ
A spring that raises the landscape where the constraints are violated; μ is its stiffness.
Multiplier, λk
The price of mass in cell k; the exchange rate between energy and that cell’s capacity.
Augmented Lagrangian
The modified landscape “energy − prices × violations + spring”, minimised in an inner loop while an outer loop updates prices and stiffness.
Gauss–Newton
Keeping only the part of the spring’s curvature built from constraint slopes; drops the constraints’ own curvature.
C⁰ / C¹ / C²
No gaps / no kinks / no jumps in curvature.
Lipschitz gradient
The slope cannot change faster than some fixed rate; the precise thing Newton’s convergence theorem needs, and what a C¹ piecewise polynomial gives for free.
Bisector
The great-circle arc separating two neighbouring cells.
Sweep rate
How fast a bisector moves when one of its sites moves; the building block of every constraint slope.
Geometric tolerance
A “close enough counts as equal” rule; each one is a small discontinuity in the objective, so they must stay at rounding scale and never stand in for known exact geometry.
Piecewise polynomial field
A density stored as one polynomial per triangle of a spherical mesh.
Powell–Sabin spline
C¹ quadratic pieces on a six-way split of each triangle, determined by value and slope at the vertices.
Bézier / Bernstein form
Writing a polynomial piece in terms of control values; nonnegative control values give a nonnegative piece.
Closure invariant
Exactness survives only if the density is polynomial on tiles bounded by the same curves the cells are made of — great circles on the sphere.
Bandwidth rule
Density detail finer than a cell only matters through its local average; choose mesh resolution from N.
